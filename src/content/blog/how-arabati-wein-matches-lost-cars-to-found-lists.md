Arabati Wein (عربتي وين؟, "Where is my car?") is a free, open source registry of vehicles looted or lost in the Sudan war. Open Source Europe (OSE) has hosted the project since November 2026. I started it in August 2026 to find my family's own car.

## The problem

When the fighting reached Khartoum, families left and their cars stayed behind. Many cars were stolen. Many others were later collected into yards by police committees and volunteers.

Those volunteers publish lists of the cars they hold. A list is a post in a Facebook group or a Telegram channel. Each line has a vehicle type and the last digits of a chassis number or a plate. Dozens of groups publish these lists, and a new list pushes the old one down the page within hours.

An owner who wants to check for one number has to read every list in every group, every day. Some people charge owners a fee to search for them.

## What the registry does

Arabati Wein collects the public posts and reads the numbers out of them. It also reads the numbers in photographed lists. A number enters the registry only if the same digits appear in the source post, so the registry does not show a number that no one published.

You search by chassis number or plate from a phone, and each result links to the original post. If the registry does not have your number, you register it. The registry then checks every new list for that number and sends you a message when the number appears.

Today the registry holds about 207,000 posts and 110,000 vehicles, and it watches 241 numbers for their owners. The code is on [GitHub](https://github.com/FirasFirari/arabati-wein) under the Apache-2.0 license. The registry is at [mycarsd.online](https://mycarsd.online/).

## What the first two recoveries taught me

On 15 November 2026, the owner of a rickshaw wrote that he had collected it. On 21 November, the owner of a Hyundai Accent wrote the same. I learned three things from them.

- **The number was already public.** Both vehicles were in a public list before their owners registered them. In both cases no one had connected the owner and the list.
- **A matching number is not proof.** Lists often print only five or six digits, and different cars share them. One early match was a different make of car. Now I ask the owner for the make first, and I tell the owner to compare the full chassis number on the car with the papers.
- **Owners expect a scam.** Several owners asked me for photos or asked who I was. The message now says that the registry is free, holds no cars, and asks for no money or documents.

My family's car is still missing. Two other families have theirs back.
